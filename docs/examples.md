### 1. Mandated Coaching Staff Limits (The Rules)
* **Is there a difference?** **No.**
* **NCAA Regulations:** Under NCAA Division I Bylaw 11, the limit on the number of "countable" on-court coaches is **identical** for both men's and women's basketball programs. 
* **Recent Changes:** Effective July 1, 2023, the NCAA Division I Council increased the limit of allowable on-court coaches. Previously, both men’s and women’s programs were capped at 4 countable coaches (1 Head Coach and 3 Assistant Coaches). The updated rules allow **6 countable coaches** (1 Head Coach and 5 Assistant Coaches, though only 3 are permitted to recruit off-campus, while the other 2 are restricted to on-court technical/tactical training). 
* **Statistical Significance:** Because the NCAA strictly regulates these limits and both divisions are capped at the exact same number, there is no difference to measure, making a statistical test inapplicable.

---

### 2. Total Program & Support Staff (In Practice)
* **Is there a difference?** **Yes, a substantial one.**
* **The Role of Support Staff:** Beyond the on-court coaches, college basketball programs employ extensive "non-coaching support staff." * **The Source of Disparity:** The NCAA does not impose a strict limit on the number of non-coaching support staff a school can hire. Historically, men's basketball programs generate exponentially more revenue through media rights, ticket sales, and donor funding, resulting in much larger operational budgets.
* **Staffing Reality:**

### Key Details and Context

* **What the Test Measures:** Under Prong 1 of Title IX's three-part participation test, an athletic department is considered compliant if the gender ratio of its varsity athletes is "substantially proportionate" to the gender ratio of its full-time undergraduate student body. While exact parity is not required, the margin of error must generally be small enough that the underrepresented gender's participation gap is not large enough to support a viable new team.
* **The "Power 5" Gender Gap:** At most major FBS football-sponsoring universities, women make up the majority of the undergraduate student body (often 53% to 58%). However, because of massive 100+ player rosters in football, the athlete population at these schools remains heavily skewed toward men. 
* **Current Legal Relevance:** This non-compliance is highly critical in light of the **_House v. NCAA_** antitrust settlement. Starting in the 2025–26 academic year, schools are permitted to share up to $20.5 million annually in revenue directly with athletes. Many Power 5 athletic departments plan to funnel roughly 90% of those funds to football and men's basketball. Because Title IX requires financial aid and benefits to be distributed proportionally to the gender makeup of a school's athletic participants, the fact that nearly 88% of these schools already fail the participation proportionality test places them at extreme risk for class-action civil rights lawsuits if they do not heavily subsidize female athletes in other ways.

### Gemini + Google Search

In the post-*House v. NCAA* settlement landscape, college athletics has transitioned from a chaotic "wild west" of unregulated Name, Image, and Likeness (NIL) deals into a structured system of **direct institutional revenue sharing** alongside traditional third-party NIL. 

Under this model, Division I schools can share up to 22% of athletic department revenues directly with athletes (capped at roughly $20.5 million to $21.3 million per school annually). 

While it initially seemed that schools with massive, multi-million-dollar football programs (primarily in the Big Ten and SEC) would completely dominate this new era, schools without large football programs—such as those in the Big East (e.g., Villanova, Marquette, St. John's) or high-performing mid-major basketball schools—have found unique ways to generate revenue and remain highly competitive. 

Non-football and mid-major programs use several key strategies to bring in enough revenue and leverage their unique positioning:

---

### 1. Exploiting the "No Football Tax" Advantage
The most significant competitive advantage for non-football schools is roster math. 
* **The Football Tax:** At a major Power Four school with an FBS football team, the athletic department must spread its $20.5+ million revenue-sharing cap across dozens of sports. Due to the sheer size and revenue-generating power of football, most of these schools allocate roughly **75% ($14M to $15M) of their pool to football**, leaving only about 15% ($3M to $4M) for men’s basketball. 
* **The Non-Football Advantage:** Because basketball-only schools do not have to fund an 85-man football roster, they can allocate a disproportionately high percentage of their revenue-sharing pool directly to men’s and women's basketball. A Big East school, for example, can easily funnel $6 million to $10 million into its basketball roster, effectively outspending SEC or Big Ten giants on the hardwood. 

### 2. Redirecting Donor Money Directly to the University
Unlike the SEC or Big Ten, which are funded by massive $100 million+ television rights deals, non-football schools cannot fund a $10–$20 million revenue-sharing budget solely from media rights. To bridge the gap, they have restructured how they use donor money:
* In the early years of NIL, boosters gave money to independent, outside "collectives" to pay players. 
* Today, schools have redirected those boosters to give **directly to the university’s athletic department**. The school then uses these donor-backed institutional funds to legally pay the athletes through the official revenue-sharing system. 

### 3. Leveraging Megadonors for Unlimited "Third-Party" NIL
While direct revenue sharing from the school is capped, **third-party commercial NIL deals are uncapped**. These deals are subject to oversight (such as reporting to the *NIL Go* clearinghouse to ensure they represent "fair market value" for actual services), but they remain a primary vehicle for player compensation.
* Non-football schools rely heavily on ultra-wealthy, basketball-centric alumni and business owners.
* For example, billionaire Mike Repole (co-founder of Bodyarmor) has poured massive resources into St. John’s University. Megadonors like Repole can fund the athletic department's revenue-sharing pool *and* use their corporate brands to sign athletes to lucrative, legitimate commercial marketing contracts.

### 4. Creating New In-House Corporate Revenue Streams
Because they lack football media revenue, basketball-centric athletic departments have had to get highly creative with corporate sponsorships and in-house monetization:
* **Jersey Patches and Uniform Sponsorships:** Selling advertising space on basketball jerseys and warmups.
* **Reorganizing Donor Incentives:** Tying preferred seating, parking, and premium game-day experiences directly to "athlete compensation" fund donations rather than general building or facility funds.
* **Aggressive Ticket and Arena Pricing:** Ticket prices for premium basketball matchups have been scaled up to directly fund the player-retention pool. 

### 5. Roster and Sport Optimization (Strategic Downsizing)
Power conferences try to be "everything to everyone," funding 20+ varsity sports. Non-football and mid-major schools are increasingly adopting a "lean" model.
* They focus their financial resources on one or two flagship sports (usually men's and women's basketball) where they have a historical brand and high fan engagement.
* Rather than trying to compete across the board, they optimize their rosters, sometimes reducing the scholarship count in non-revenue sports or transitioning certain programs to club status to free up millions of dollars to keep their basketball programs elite.

### Summary
The post-NIL/revenue-sharing landscape has actually created a survival-of-the-focussed environment. Schools without football may not have the highest overall athletic budgets, but by **avoiding the massive financial drain of football, redirecting collective donors into the university, and focusing their resources entirely on basketball**, they are keeping the playing field remarkably level.
